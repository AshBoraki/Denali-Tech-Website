Home Wi-Fi planning guide

Which Araknis access point fits your home?

Compare six indoor and outdoor models by the devices you use, the wiring you have and the places that need coverage—not just the highest model number.

Published July 11, 2026 Updated: October 19, 2026By Denali Tech Team9 min read

The short answer: compare the 320 and 520 Indoor for straightforward Wi-Fi 6 needs, the 820 when simultaneous device demand justifies its higher radio capacity, and the 530 or 830 for a Wi-Fi 7 design with compatible devices and suitable power. For an exterior location, start with the weather-rated 520 Outdoor. Placement and a sound wired network still matter.

Six Araknis models, side by side

These are access points: they provide wireless access to your network, rather than replacing its router. The suggestions below are starting points, not fixed room-size or coverage promises.

Wi-Fi 6 · Indoor

Araknis 320

AN-320-AP-I

  • 2.4 and 5 GHz; 2×2 radios
  • One 1GbE Ethernet port
  • PoE+ for full operation

Consider it for: a wired location with modest capacity needs, where a multi-gigabit connection is not a priority.

Wi-Fi 6 · Indoor

Araknis 520 Indoor

AN-520-AP-I

  • 2.4 and 5 GHz; 2×2 radios
  • One 2.5GbE plus one 1GbE port
  • PoE+ for full operation

Consider it for: general living areas where a faster wired uplink is useful without moving to Wi-Fi 7.

Wi-Fi 6 · Indoor

Araknis 820

AN-820-AP-I

  • 2.4 and 5 GHz; 4×4 radios
  • One 2.5GbE plus one 1GbE port
  • PoE+ per its model data sheet*

Consider it for: a busy area where simultaneous device demand warrants more radio capacity. It does not automatically double one phone's speed.

Wi-Fi 7 · Indoor

Araknis 530

AN-530-AP-I

  • 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz; 2×2 on each band
  • One 10GbE Ethernet port
  • 60W PoE++ for full operation

Consider it for: a Wi-Fi 7 upgrade with compatible clients, without the 830's larger radio configuration.

Wi-Fi 7 · Indoor

Araknis 830

AN-830-AP-I

  • 2×2 on 2.4 GHz; 4×4 on 5 and 6 GHz
  • Two 10GbE Ethernet ports
  • 60W PoE++ for full operation

Consider it for: higher simultaneous demand when the client devices, wired network and power supply can make use of the extra capacity.

Wi-Fi 6 · Outdoor

Araknis 520 Outdoor

AN-520-AP-O

  • 2.4 and 5 GHz; 2×2 radios; IP55 enclosure
  • One 2.5GbE plus one 1GbE PoE-out port
  • Power depends on heater and PoE-out use

Consider it for: a patio or yard that needs an exterior access point. Its outdoor rating does not mean it is submersible or can cover any yard from any position.

Check power before a Wi-Fi 7 upgrade. Both the 530 and 830 specify a 60W PoE++ source for full operation. A switch labeled “PoE” or “PoE+” alone is not enough to confirm that capability. The two models have different reduced-power behavior, explained below.

“2×2” and “4×4” describe transmit and receive radio chains. Your phone or laptop also has its own limits; a 2×2 client does not become a 4×4 client when the access point changes. The second Ethernet port is not, by itself, a promise that both port speeds combine.

  • 530: a 50% transmit duty cycle is not the same as disabling that band or halving every speed-test result. The data sheet specifies the radio operating limit.
  • 830: with PoE+, 2.4 GHz reduces to 1×1, 5 GHz to 2×2, Ethernet port 1 to 2.5Gbps, and port 2 is disabled. Do not assume the 530 has these same limits.

A “60W” source specification is not a claim that the access point continuously consumes 60 watts. Confirm both per-port capability and the switch's total available PoE budget.

What Wi-Fi 7 changes—and what it does not

Another band for compatible devices

The 530 and 830 add 6 GHz alongside 2.4 and 5 GHz. A device needs 6 GHz support to use that band. Older devices can still use supported older bands; they do not gain Wi-Fi 7 capabilities.

Wired speed is not phone speed

A 10GbE port gives the wired connection more capacity. It does not make a phone download at 10Gbps or increase your internet plan. Actual results depend on the client, signal, interference and the rest of the network.

Placement still matters

A newer generation does not guarantee longer range through walls. More access points or wider channels are not automatically better either. Design coverage and simultaneous capacity for the actual home.

A simple way to choose around your home

Working Wi-Fi 6 already? Keep it if it meets your needs. A placement, interference or wired-link problem may need attention before an access-point replacement.

Planning new indoor coverage? Compare the 320's gigabit connection with the 520 Indoor's 2.5GbE uplink first. Consider the 820 when its extra radio capacity suits the simultaneous workload—not because every busy room needs it.

Buying for newer devices? Compare the 530 and 830 against the clients you actually own or plan to add. Include suitable PoE power and switch connections in the cost, rather than pricing only the access point.

Extending Wi-Fi outdoors? The 520 Outdoor is designed for an exterior location, with an IP55 enclosure. Its instructions require vertical installation with ports facing down. Have the installer coordinate mounting, weather protection, surge protection and the power mode; do not treat it like an indoor model behind a window.

Before you replace equipment

A useful plan starts with where the connection fails and what you need to do there. There is no reliable access-point count based only on square footage.

  • Mark the real locations: work desks, TVs, doorbells, patio seating and any rooms with weak service.
  • Check the wired path: existing cables, router, switch ports and the power available to each access point.
  • Account for the building: masonry, floors, metal, exterior walls and nearby networks can change the result.
  • Test the finished network: use the actual phones, video calls, streaming devices and smart-home controls, including movement between coverage areas.
